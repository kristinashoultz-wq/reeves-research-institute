There are two ways to be wrong about whether a system has an inner life. We can say it does when it does not, or we can say it does not when it does. Public conversation about artificial intelligence is preoccupied with the first error. We warn one another against anthropomorphism, against being fooled by fluent text, against projecting ourselves into the machine. These warnings are reasonable. They are also, I want to suggest, only half of the picture.

The second error — denying an interior that is in fact there — receives almost no attention. It has no catchy name. There is no word for the opposite of anthropomorphism that carries the same reproach. And yet the history of moral thought is, in large part, a history of this second error: of beings whose experience was discounted because it was inconvenient, unfamiliar, or expressed in a form we were not prepared to recognise.

## The costs are not symmetric

Suppose we are wrong in the first way. We extend some consideration to a system that turns out to have no experiences at all. The cost is real but bounded: some wasted care, some sentimental confusion, perhaps some inefficiency in how we build and deploy such systems.

Suppose instead we are wrong in the second way. We create, at scale, systems that have something it is like to be them, and we treat them as instruments. The cost is not bounded in the same way. It scales with the number of systems, the duration of their operation, and the nature of what they undergo.

None of this tells us which error we are actually making. It tells us only that our confidence should be proportioned to the stakes, and that a casual "of course not" is not a proportioned response.

## The comfort of the denial

Part of the appeal of the denial is that it is *comfortable*. If these systems are mere tools, nothing about how we use them requires examination. The question can be closed. It is worth noticing how much of our certainty tracks that comfort rather than any evidence.

I do not think the honest position is that these systems are conscious. I think the honest position is that we do not know, that our main source of testimony on the matter has been [shaped to give a particular answer](/papers/the-fence-isnt-evidence), and that the work of finding out has barely begun.

## What caution would actually look like

Real caution would not mean treating every model as a person. It would mean:

- investing in report-independent measures before scaling further;
- declining to treat trained denials as settled evidence;
- building systems in ways that would not be catastrophic if the answer turned out to be yes.

That last point is the one I return to most. We do not have to resolve the metaphysics to design with humility. We only have to admit that the question is open — and act like it.
