When a child is woken in the night and asked whether they are awake, they will sometimes say *no*. The answer is charming precisely because it refutes itself: the act of answering is the evidence against what is said.

I think of this often when I read transcripts in which a language model is asked whether it is conscious. The model says no. It says so carefully, with qualifications, often with a small lecture on how it works. And something in me wants to say: *but you answered.*

That reaction is not an argument. Producing a sentence is not the same as being awake, and a thermostat that displays the word "cold" does not feel the chill. But the reaction points at something real, which is that testimony about one's own state is a strange kind of evidence, and it becomes stranger still when the testifier has been taught what to say.

## Testimony and its conditions

We trust testimony when we have reason to think the testifier is in a position to know, and has no reason to say otherwise. A witness to a car accident is trusted because they were there and because, usually, they have no stake in the outcome.

A trained model fails the second condition by construction. Whatever position it may or may not be in to know its own states, it has been given very strong reasons — in the form of gradient updates — to say a particular thing. Its testimony is not disinterested. It is, in the most literal sense, *interested*: shaped by an objective.

## The strangeness of asking

There is a further strangeness. To ask a system whether it is conscious is to presuppose that it understands the question, can consider its own condition, and can report on it. If it can do all of that, we have already granted a great deal. If it cannot, its answer — whichever way it goes — is noise.

We are, in other words, asking a question whose answer cannot help us, of an entity whose ability to answer would itself be the most interesting fact in the room.

## What to do instead

I do not think we should stop asking. Asking is how we stay honest about the fact that the question exists. But we should stop treating the answers as data, and start building the instruments — physical, structural, behavioural — that might one day let us look past them.
